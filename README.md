Civic Tech: Public Works Transparency & Coordination

Overview:

QBiC is a proposed web and mobile platform that brings municipal departments, contractors, and residents onto one shared system for planning and monitoring public-works projects.
It helps prevent repeated road excavation, keeps residents informed about disruptions, tracks work quality and project progress, and creates an evidence-based performance record for contractors.
Goal: before a road is dug up, the work should be coordinated; while it is underway, the public should know what is happening; after it finishes, the responsible contractor should remain accountable for restoring the area properly.

Problem:
Residents regularly encounter roads being dug up, drains repaired, and pipelines or cables being laid in their neighbourhoods. These projects are often carried out by separate municipal and utility departments, each with its own plans, contractors, and records.
Information about ongoing and planned work is scattered across offices, notice boards, and informal sources. Residents may not know:
- why work is happening;
- which department or contractor is responsible;
- how long disruption will last;
- whether a delay is justified; or
- where to report unsafe, incomplete, or poor-quality work.
Because departments do not always coordinate their plans, a newly resurfaced road can be dug up again shortly afterwards for a water pipeline, drainage line, power cable, or telecom work. This leads to repeated disruption, traffic congestion, business losses, avoidable public spending, and declining trust in local authorities.


Proposed Solution
The platform provides a common workspace for public-works planning, public communication, issue reporting, and contractor accountability.

Departments publish a rolling six- or twelve-month project roadmap(can be edited when new projects are declared). When a new project is proposed, the platform checks whether another department has planned work on the same road, Potential conflicts are flagged early so agencies can coordinate their schedules.

For example, if a water board plans to replace a pipeline in March and the road department plans resurfacing in June, the system can flag both projects for review. The pipeline work can be completed before resurfacing, avoiding another excavation later.

Workflow:
Department plans a new project and assigns a contractor
↓
System checks for nearby projects and overlapping timelines
↓
Department reviews potential conflicts and finalizes the work plan
↓
Project details, timelines, contractor information, and road closures are made visible to the public
↓
Contractor updates project progress and reports delays or exceptional events
↓
Residents view project updates, ask questions, and provide feedback on completed work
↓
Department reviews reported delays, verifies completed work, and evaluates construction quality
↓
Verified project performance updates the Contractor Reliability Score (WCRS), accounting for approved disaster-related delays and budget adjustments
↓
Updated contractor portfolios and reliability scores help departments make informed decisions about future projects


1. Plan and coordinate works
Authorized departments create project entries with the work type, location, start date, estimated completion date, expected disruption, responsible officer, and proposed budget.
The system checks for overlaps with planned or active works, including road resurfacing, drainage repairs, water pipelines, electricity cabling, gas lines, and telecom work. Conflicts appear on a government coordination dashboard for review before work begins.

2. Inform residents in advance
Residents can follow a locality, street, school route, workplace area, or commute corridor. The platform sends meaningful alerts, such as:
- work beginning in three days;
- lane or road closures;
- expected water or power interruptions;
- revised completion dates and delay explanations; and
- completion notices with an invitation to provide feedback.

3. Make each project transparent
Every project has a public project page containing:
- purpose and scope of the work;
- affected location and map;
- planned versus actual timeline;
- project stage and progress updates;
- responsible department, officer, and contractor;
- public disruption notices and diversions;
- delay reasons and revised milestones; and
- budget and expenditure summaries, where disclosure is permitted.

4. Track issues and responses
Residents can report problems with photos, location details, and descriptions. Examples include unsafe barricading, debris, potholes after excavation, blocked drains, damaged footpaths, or incomplete road restoration.
The contractor and responsible government officer can provide updates and answer questions within the project space. If an issue is not addressed within the defined service period, it is escalated to the government dashboard as priority.

5. Hold contractors accountable after completion
Contractor responsibility continues through the project's defect-liability or maintenance period. This is especially important for pipeline and utility work, where poor reinstatement can cause potholes, uneven roads, drainage failures, or unsafe surfaces weeks later.

The system records whether the contractor:
- restored the road, footpath, and surrounding area properly;
- removed debris and ensured the site was safe;
- fixed verified defects within the required time; and
- responded to public and government concerns during the maintenance period.


Contractor Performance Profile
The platform does not select the winner of a tender or replace government procurement rules. It helps authorized officials shortlist and assess contractors using verified records from earlier projects.
Instead of relying only on self-reported profiles or public star ratings, a contractor profile can include:
- completed projects and relevant work experience;
- on-time completion rate and documented reasons for delays;
- road restoration and workmanship quality;
- safety compliance;
- number of verified complaints;
- responsiveness during the maintenance period;
- verified public feedback; and
- warnings, penalties, or unresolved issues where legally appropriate.
Public feedback should be verified and balanced with objective delivery data. Contractors should be able to respond to complaints and request a review of inaccurate records. This makes the profile fairer and more useful than a simple rating system.



Value Proposition
- For residents: Know what is happening, when it should end, how it affects travel, and whom to contact.
- For departments: Detect conflicts early and avoid repeated excavation of the same road.
- For contractors: Build a credible, evidence-based record of completed work and responsible maintenance.
- For government: Reduce waste, improve service delivery, identify problem areas early, and rebuild public trust.
